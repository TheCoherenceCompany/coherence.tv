﻿Events : Market Segment


  

	Market Segment full description : Organizers of purpose-driven in-person Events (only pre-activation)  
	1. TL;DR chatGPT
The market segment is organizers of purpose-driven in-person events where the real value is not just content, but meaningful connection: conferences, summits, retreats, professional gatherings, ecosystem convenings, and business events. These organizers know networking matters, but current tools barely help people arrive prepared, find the right people, or turn the event into a living network. The Coherence Company’s offering helps activate the network before the event, generate richer participant insight, improve matchmaking, create shareable content, and make the whole gathering feel more intentional, intelligent, and alive.
This also fits the broader Coherence Company thesis: building AI-native systems that increase trust, learning, and coordination between humans and intelligent agents.

2. Market Segment Overview
The core segment is in-person event organizers whose events depend on high-quality human connection.
This includes:
* Business conferences
* Industry summits
* Innovation gatherings
* Retreats
* Investment summits
* Founder/investor events
* Scientific or professional conferences
* Regenerative, systemic, impact, or ecosystem-building gatherings
* Large-scale events such as Web Summit-style conferences where networking becomes chaotic
The target customer is likely the event organizer, curator, community lead, conference producer, or ecosystem convenor.
This segment is distinct from generic event software buyers because the event’s value does not come only from ticketing, logistics, stage programming, or speaker management. The real value is in who meets whom, what conversations happen, and what collective intelligence emerges before, during, and after the gathering.
The strongest fit is not ordinary corporate events where people passively attend. It is events where participants arrive with professional, strategic, intellectual, investment, learning, or ecosystem intentions.

3. Core Problems and Pain Points
Surface-level problems
Event organizers struggle with:
* Weak pre-event engagement
* Generic webinars that do not meaningfully activate participants
* Poor participant profiles
* Low-quality networking
* Attendees not knowing who they should meet
* Missed connections
* Difficulty understanding what participants actually care about
* Limited shareable content before the event
* Participants arriving underprepared
* Networking apps that feel dead, transactional, or profile-heavy
The transcript is sharp here: “better than nothing” is not good enough. A pre-event webinar with 40 people is technically engagement, but it does not create much relational energy. It is a beige biscuit wearing a party hat.
Deeper underlying tensions
The deeper problem is that events promise connection but often leave connection to chance.
For small events, people can still meet most participants. For large events, the probability of meeting the right person becomes “lottery ticket level.” That is a structural failure, not a UX inconvenience.
There is also a mismatch between participant intention and event infrastructure. People attend with serious goals: buy, sell, invest, learn, partner, discover, recruit, contribute. But the event rarely helps them clarify those intentions in advance.
The unspoken pain: organizers sell transformation, but the actual human network is under-activated.

6. Benefits of the Startup’s Offering
Functional benefits
The product enables participants to have one, two, or three guided online conversations before the event. From those conversations, it can generate:
* Fresh participant profiles
* Thematic summaries
* Matchmaking insights
* Intentions and needs
* Suggested people to meet
* Content that can be published or shared
* Event-level sensemaking for organizers
* Potential AI event agent or avatar for event information and guidance
The key product insight is excellent: nobody wants to manually create another profile. But people will talk, especially if the conversation itself is useful.
Strategic benefits
For organizers, the offering helps reveal what the event’s audience actually wants before everyone arrives. That means the event can become more adaptive.
Example from the transcript: if many participants care about Topic X and the agenda barely covers it, the organizer can adjust programming.
That is not networking software. That is demand intelligence for live event curation.
Financial or growth benefits
The clearest commercial benefits are:
* More participant engagement
* Better event marketing through authentic shared conversations
* Higher perceived event value
* Stronger repeat attendance
* Better sponsor outcomes through sharper attendee insight
* Potential upsell into premium matchmaking or intelligence layers
* More defensible differentiation for event organizers
The “viral communication” angle is important. Participants talking about why they are attending is much more valuable than generic reposts of event marketing.
Cultural or human benefits
Participants get:
* Less wasted time
* Better matches
* More clarity about why they are attending
* Easier professional visibility
* A lightweight way to publish thoughtful content
* Deeper conversations once they meet in person
* A sense of being seen beyond a stale LinkedIn profile
The strongest human benefit is that the product helps people move from first-contact small talk to second- or third-conversation depth before the event even starts.
Long-term ecosystem benefits
For ecosystem-building events, the product can activate second-degree connections.
Someone may not be relevant to you directly, but they may know exactly who you should meet. If this happens before the event, people have time to act on it.
This is a powerful insight: the product does not only match people; it warms the network graph before physical convergence.

8. Objections and Risks
Likely objections include:
“Will participants actually use it?”
This is the biggest adoption risk. Event organizers will fear low participation. The product must prove it can create enough value from lightweight engagement.
“Is this just another event app?”
This is dangerous. If positioned as an event networking app, it enters a crowded, tired category. It should be positioned as pre-event network activation and sensemaking, not another badge-scanning circus.
“Will people want their conversations published?”
The transcript assumes public events where conversations can be published. That may work for some communities, but consent and privacy must be explicit. Some participants will prefer private or semi-private sharing.
“Does this create operational burden?”
Event teams are often overwhelmed. If setup requires too much facilitation, custom design, onboarding, moderation, or support, adoption will suffer.
“Is the AI actually useful?”
The AI cannot feel like decorative glitter. It must produce better matches, better profiles, better insights, or better content.
“What is the ROI?”
Organizers may like the idea but still struggle to justify budget unless the value maps clearly to ticket sales, retention, sponsor value, attendee satisfaction, or premium experience.

9. Best-Fit Customer Profile
The ideal first customer is:
An organizer of a high-intent, relationship-driven event with 100 to 5,000 participants, where attendees come to meet collaborators, investors, partners, clients, experts, or peers, and where the organizer cares about community quality, not just attendance volume.
Best early buyers are likely:
* Innovation summit organizers
* Investment and founder event organizers
* Regenerative/ecosystem convenors
* AI/business transformation conference teams
* Professional community leaders
* Retreat and leadership gathering organizers
* Curated conference brands with premium audiences
The likely buyer is someone with both programming responsibility and community responsibility. Titles may include founder, event director, community lead, ecosystem lead, head of partnerships, or conference curator.
They buy first because the pain is visible, the value is strategic, and they are already trying to create something more meaningful than a room with lanyards.

10. Poor-Fit Customers
Poor-fit customers include:
* Purely logistical events where networking does not matter
* Low-cost mass events where attendees have weak intent
* Internal corporate events with rigid agendas and little appetite for experimentation
* Event organizers who only care about ticketing and stage production
* Audiences unlikely to participate in pre-event conversations
* Events with highly sensitive topics unless privacy architecture is mature
* Organizers looking for a generic event app replacement
* One-off events with no community or repeat engagement ambition
Also poor fit: events where the organizer wants AI novelty but does not actually care about participant relationships. That customer will waste time and request shiny nonsense.

11. Messaging Angle
Main promise
Turn your event audience into an activated network before anyone enters the room.
Alternative:
Help participants find the right people, clarify their intentions, and arrive ready for better conversations.
Tone of voice
The tone should be intelligent, grounded, and practical. Avoid mystical “collective consciousness” language in front of mainstream event buyers. Save the deeper philosophy for aligned communities.
Use language like:
* Pre-event network activation
* Better participant matching
* Fresh intent-based profiles
* Event sensemaking
* More valuable networking
* Warm the network before the room opens
* Help attendees arrive prepared
* Reveal what your audience actually cares about
* Turn conversations into insight and content
Avoid:
* “AI magic”
* “Revolutionize events”
* “Replace networking”
* “Autonomous agentic social graph”
* “Consciousness layer for gatherings”
* “Serendipity engine” unless used carefully
* Anything that sounds like surveillance
The best language is concrete, almost modest. Let the implications be large; do not make the words wear platform shoes.

12. Segment Summary
This segment is attractive because events are already spending money to create connection, but the connection layer remains badly underbuilt. Organizers know networking matters, participants know they waste time, and both sides feel the gap between who is in the room and what actually happens.
Now is the right time because AI can turn lightweight conversation into useful profiles, matching, content, and collective insight without forcing participants to fill out another dead form. The market is also ready for events that feel more personalized, intelligent, and community-driven.
To win this segment, The Coherence Company must prove three things:
1. Participants will actually engage before the event.
2. The resulting matches and insights are meaningfully better than existing networking tools.
3. Organizers can see measurable value in engagement, satisfaction, content, sponsor value, or repeat attendance.
The strategic wedge is strong: start with pre-event conversation and network activation for high-intent gatherings. Do not initially sell the whole civilization-scale coordination thesis. That may be true, but investors and customers need the door handle before they admire the cathedral.
