Market Segment Full Definition: Civil Society Engagement and Governance
1. TL;DR
This market segment is municipal-level civil society governance: local governments, elected representatives, citizens, civic organisations, local businesses, associations, and community groups trying to make democratic participation more continuous, useful, and action-oriented. The problem is that current local democracy often gives citizens a (oftentimes “pseudo”) voice only at election time, while elected officials struggle to understand nuanced public priorities beyond the loudest, closest, or most organised voices. The Coherence Company can serve this segment by using Coherence Conversations to help communities listen to themselves, hold elected officials accountable, define future priorities, find people with shared concerns, and self-organise around practical local action; eventually with or without elected officials.

2. Market Segment Overview
This segment sits at the intersection of local governance, civic engagement, participatory democracy, community organising, and civil society coordination.
The most immediate target context is the municipality, city, town, parish, neighbourhood, or local territory. The conversation deliberately starts at municipal level because national governance is too large and abstract as a first use case. A municipality (or even parts of it) is small enough to test, politically concrete enough to matter, and close enough to people’s daily lives for participation to feel real.
The potential customers and participants include several groups:
* Municipal governments and mayors who want to understand their population better, improve accountability, and create more intelligent participation.
* Elected representatives or candidates who want to show progress against their electoral programme and listen to emerging citizen priorities.
* Citizens and residents who want more than voting every four years and would like to influence, organise, or contribute to local life.
* Civil society organisations, including NGOs, associations, sports clubs, cultural groups, social support organisations, youth organisations, environmental groups, and community networks.
* Local businesses and business networks, especially when local economic life is part of the territory’s wellbeing.
* Private brands or sponsors who may support conversations around specific themes such as sport, youth, food supply, community development, loneliness, sustainability, or local culture.
* Individual subscribers or “My Coherence” account holders, who may eventually have the ability to initiate or participate in civic Coherence Conversations themselves.
This segment is distinct from broader “government technology” or “civic tech” because the core value is not simply digitising services, collecting complaints, or running online surveys. The deeper opportunity is to help a local community make sense of itself.
The segment is also distinct from traditional political campaigning. It is not primarily about persuading voters, winning elections, or producing party messaging. It is about creating better listening, better accountability, better collective understanding, and better conditions for local action.
A strong first customer would likely be a forward-looking municipality with a mayor or civic leadership team willing to pilot a new form of structured citizen conversation. This person or team must be willing to take some political risk: opening a more transparent listening process means exposing what has not been done, what citizens care about, and where the administration may be misaligned with public priorities.
A reasonable assumption is that the first pilots should happen in places with enough civic trust and leadership courage to try something new, but not necessarily in already highly sophisticated participatory-democracy environments. The offering may be especially valuable where existing participation is weak, fragmented, politicised, or dominated by a few organised voices.

3. Core Problems and Pain Points
Surface-level problems
The first visible problem is that citizens are rarely engaged (even less in a structured way) between elections.
In many municipalities, parties or independent movements create electoral programmes organised around themes such as housing, senior care, healthcare, education, childhood, sport, green spaces, infrastructure, waste management, water supply, cultural life, entrepreneurship, food support, and social inclusion, among others. These programmes are formally important, but in practice very few citizens read them carefully. Even fewer track whether the promises are being delivered. And, even if they would…, no agency is there.
This creates a weak accountability loop. Elected officials may govern for four years with limited structured scrutiny other than from political opposition. At the end of the mandate, opponents may accuse them of not delivering, but that debate often becomes political theatre rather than public learning or meaningful action.
Citizens also lack a clear way to express what matters to them now. The programme under which a mayor was elected may not fully reflect the community’s evolving needs. New concerns emerge: loneliness, local environmental damage, post-disaster recovery, youth activities, elderly care, educational alternatives, food resilience, public spaces, cultural life, or sports development. These concerns often remain scattered, informal, or trapped inside small groups.
Municipal leaders face their own pain. They may want to understand the community, but they often hear from whoever is most vocal, most connected, most politically organised, or closest to power. The “person having barbecues with the mayor” may have more influence than quieter citizens with equally (or sometimes who knows better) valid insights.
There is also a coordination problem across institutions. In moments of crisis or shared local need, many organisations may be present, but they often struggle to cooperate quickly. Egos, budgets, unclear responsibilities, institutional boundaries, political agendas, among other constraints, can block effective collaboration.
Deeper underlying tensions
The deeper tension is that modern democratic systems are trying to operate at a level of social complexity they were not originally designed for.
The conversation briefly names the ancient democratic model as a useful contrast: a small city-state, face-to-face participation, and a narrow definition of who counted as “a citizen”. Today, democratic systems are stretched across large populations, complex institutions, and fast-changing social needs. Voting remains essential, but voting alone does not provide enough continuous intelligence for good governance; actually, far from it.
At local level, this produces a gap between representation and participation. Citizens elect representatives, but then often become passive observers until the next election. Elected officials receive a formal mandate, but may lack ongoing, nuanced, trusted, effective feedback from the community.
Another deeper problem is the limitation of government as mainly a tax collector, fund distributor, and rule setter. Municipalities often try to solve problems by allocating budgets, writing regulations, or delivering services directly. These are important functions, but they are insufficient. Many local problems require collaboration between citizens, businesses, associations, schools, NGOs, public agencies, cultural groups, and informal networks.
The more ambitious opportunity is to help local government become a facilitator of collaboration: not only doing things for people, but helping people find each other, trust each other, and act together.
There is also a risk of politicisation. Antonio’s reference to the Portuguese Social Forum in the early 2000’s is important: civic spaces can be captured by political actors who use them to push partisan agendas rather than listen or even less acting honestly. That creates distrust and drives ordinary citizens away. Any Coherence Company offering in this segment must therefore protect the space from becoming another battlefield for political manipulation.
The emotional pain underneath all this is disempowerment. People may care about their community, but they do not know where to begin, who else cares, how to be heard, or how to turn concern into action. The civic muscle has weakened in many places. The offer must help rebuild it without pretending that participation will be easy or automatically representative.

4. Values and Motivations
This segment cares about better governance, but governance here should be understood broadly. It is not only the work of elected officials. It is the way a community senses, decides, coordinates, and, very importantly, acts.
The most important values include:
Accountability. Citizens want elected officials to be answerable for what they promised. Municipal leaders who are serious about delivery may also want a clearer way to show progress.
Efficacy. Antonio names efficiency and efficacy as key ideas. The goal is not participation for its own sake. The goal is better results with less waste, lower friction, and clearer priorities.
Voice. People want to feel that their concerns can be heard even if they are not politically connected, institutionally powerful, or naturally loud.
Nuance. Local governance cannot be reduced to yes/no questions or noisy opinion polls. Communities need ways to express complexity: trade-offs, priorities, lived experiences, emerging tensions, and different perspectives.
Common good. The strongest version of this segment is not about individual complaint management. It is about helping people think and act together around shared local wellbeing.
Self-organisation. Citizens do not only want to speak to power. Some want to find the others and do things themselves: organise around loneliness, education, environmental recovery, food supply, sport, youth culture, care, or local regeneration, among so many others.
Pragmatism. The offering must be grounded. This segment will not be served by abstract democratic theory alone. It needs concrete processes that help people listen, decide, coordinate, and act.
Trust. Any civic engagement platform must be trusted by citizens, public officials, and civil society actors. It must feel fair, transparent, and non-manipulative.
Pluralism. Civil society includes individuals, associations, NGOs, businesses, local networks, and public institutions. The system must make room for difference without flattening everyone into one ideological position.
The future this segment is moving toward is not necessarily a complete replacement of current democracy. The near-term vision is more modest and more useful: make the current system work better by adding continuous listening, clearer accountability, collective sensemaking, and practical pathways for community action.

5. Desired Outcomes
Success for this segment is not simply “more engagement”. Engagement can be noisy, shallow, or performative. The desired outcome is better civic intelligence leading to better local action.
The transformation can be described in four connected tracks.
1. From electoral promises to visible accountability
Municipal programmes already exist, but they are not usually alive in public consciousness. A Coherence process could help extract the main axes of an electoral programme and create structured conversations around them.
For example:
* What was promised on housing?
* What was promised on elder care?
* What was promised on education?
* What was promised on green spaces?
* What was promised on infrastructure?
* What has been delivered?
* What remains incomplete?
* What is blocked, delayed, or changed?
* How do citizens experience the progress?
This gives elected officials a way to communicate progress and gives citizens a way to understand the mandate beyond political slogans.
2. From top-down programmes to citizen-informed future priorities
Citizens may care about issues that were not central in the current programme. A Coherence process could help surface what people now believe should matter in the next mandate.
This does not mean the current administration must abandon its elected programme immediately. Budgets, commitments, and legitimacy matter. But it creates a living intelligence layer for future planning.
Over time, this could help shape the agenda of future candidates, parties, independent movements, and local civic coalitions.
3. From isolated concern to community self-organisation
The most original civic value is not just telling government what to do. It is helping citizens discover shared interests and organise around them.
Someone may care about loneliness among elderly people. Another may care about alternative education for children. Others may care about sports access, local culture, accessible housing, environmental recovery, youth participation, food supply, or support after a natural disaster.
The Coherence Company’s “find the others” principle becomes especially relevant here. The platform can help residents discover who else is interested in the same issue, what capacities exist locally, and what small actions could begin.
4. From government as provider to government as collaboration facilitator
A thriving city or region is not created only through public spending. Municipal government can have far greater impact if it helps local actors collaborate.
This includes citizens, businesses, NGOs, schools, cultural organisations, social services, sports clubs, neighbourhood groups, and informal initiatives.
The desired outcome is a municipality that becomes better at asking:
* Who is already doing useful work?
* Who needs to be connected?
* Where are resources stuck?
* Which organisations are duplicating effort?
* Which quieter groups are invisible?
* What partnerships could solve problems more effectively than isolated programmes?
The long-term transformation is a shift from democracy as periodic representation to governance as continuous, structured, community-wide sensemaking and action.

6. Benefits of The Coherence Company’s Offering
Functional benefits
The Coherence Company can provide a structured process through which citizens, elected officials, organisations, and local stakeholders participate in guided Coherence Conversations.
These conversations can generate:
* Thematic maps of community concerns.
* Summaries of what citizens care about.
* Sensemaking around electoral programme commitments.
* Progress conversations around municipal priorities.
* Citizen-defined agendas for future mandates.
* Interest maps showing who cares about which topics.
* Matching between citizens, organisations, businesses, and initiatives.
* Topic-specific civic conversations, such as sport, youth, culture, loneliness, housing, animal care and protection, food supply, environmental recovery, education, elder care.
* Outputs that can be shared with municipal leaders, citizens, sponsors, and civil society organisations.
The functional advantage over a survey is that people can speak in richer, more human ways. The advantage over a town hall is that more voices can be included, including quieter voices, and the resulting intelligence can be synthesised more systematically.
Strategic benefits
For municipalities, the offering improves local intelligence. It helps leaders understand what people actually experience, not only what formal reports or political allies tell them.
It can also increase legitimacy. A mayor or council willing to open this kind of process can signal seriousness about listening and accountability.
For citizens, the offering creates a pathway from concern to contribution and to implementation. It says: your voice can be heard, your priorities can be connected to others, and your interest can become part of a wider pattern of local action.
For The Coherence Company, this segment expresses the broader thesis clearly: AI should help people coordinate and govern better together, not merely automate administrative tasks.
Financial or growth benefits
The commercial model is not fully settled in the transcript, so the following should be treated as a set of plausible assumptions rather than confirmed strategy.
Possible paying customers include:
1. Municipalities
 A municipality could pay for a local Coherence Conversation process around governance, programme accountability, citizen priorities, or a specific civic theme.
2. Thematic corporate sponsors
 A sports brand might support a conversation about the future of sport in a municipality or region. A food retailer might support community development, food support, or local wellbeing conversations. A youth-oriented brand might support youth futures or education-related conversations.
3. Local organisations, associations, federations, or networks
 These may have smaller budgets, but they could sponsor or co-host issue-specific civic processes.
4. Individual citizens through a B2C model
 If people hold personal “My Coherence" individual accounts, they may gain the ability to initiate, organise, or participate in civic conversations as part of their membership.
5. Hybrid funding models
 A civic conversation could be funded through a municipality, supplemented by sponsorship, supported by foundations, and activated by citizens.
Financial benefits for customers may include better allocation of public resources, stronger programme prioritisation, reduced duplication among local organisations, better crisis response, more effective public consultation, and stronger civic trust.
These benefits must be proven carefully. Municipal buyers will need to see that the process leads to actionable insight, not merely beautiful summaries. Discussion and alignment are naturally important; but ultimately implementation is (almost) everything. Implementation, Action, tangible results, that’s in the DNA of The Coherence Company.
Cultural or human benefits
The human benefit is significant. Citizens may feel less isolated in their concerns. People who care about the same issue can find one another. Quieter voices can become visible. Local identity can become more participatory and less passive.
The offering may also soften the distance between citizens and elected officials. Not by eliminating disagreement, but by creating a more structured and respectful way to surface it.
For elected leaders, it can reduce the burden of trying to understand everything through informal channels. For citizens, it can reduce the feeling that politics is something that happens elsewhere, by others (and many times perceived as “for others benefit alone”), every four years.
Long-term ecosystem benefits
Over time, this could create a new layer of local civic infrastructure.
A municipality could develop a living map of community priorities, civic actors, projects, concerns, and capacities. Intermunicipal collaborations could, and would naturally, emerge. Regional and/or Bioregional initiatives could be supported. Local governance could become more adaptive, more relational, and more capable of responding to complex challenges.
The long-term ecosystem benefit is not only better consultation. It is greater local agency.
People do not merely express preferences. They begin to see themselves as part of a living civic system, with others they can act alongside.

8. Objections and Risks
“Will this become politically manipulated?”
This is one of the biggest risks. Civic spaces can be captured by political actors trying to steer the conversation. The process must be designed to reduce manipulation, make influence visible, and protect genuine citizen voice.
“Will citizens actually participate?”
Participation cannot be assumed. Many communities do not have a strong culture of civic engagement. The process must be easy, meaningful, and clearly useful. It should not feel like homework for democracy.
“Will this expose elected officials to criticism?”
Yes. That is part of the point. A municipality that funds this must be prepared for honest feedback. The offer should frame this not as reputational risk alone, but as a way to build trust through transparency.
“Is this replacing representative democracy?”
The answer should be no. The near-term positioning is to improve the current system, not overthrow it. The offering adds better listening, accountability, and civic coordination around existing democratic structures.
“How representative is the output?”
This is a serious issue. The people who participate may not fully represent the population. The product must be careful not to claim total democratic legitimacy. It can say: this reveals structured insight from participants and helps broaden participation beyond the usual voices.
“Is AI making political judgements?”
The AI must not appear to decide what citizens should think or what politicians should do. Its role should be synthesis, pattern recognition, matching, summarisation, and support for sensemaking. Human governance remains human.
“Who owns the data?”
Civic data is sensitive. Citizens must know what is recorded, how it is used, who can access it, and whether outputs are public, private, anonymised, or attributable.
“Will this add burden to already stretched municipal teams?”
Municipalities often lack spare capacity. The offer must include clear process design and support. If it requires too much internal administration, adoption will suffer.
“What is the ROI?”
Public-sector ROI is not always financial, but buyers will still need evidence. The Coherence Company must show value in terms of better decisions, stronger legitimacy, clearer priorities, more effective collaboration, and potentially reduced waste.
“Could this raise expectations we cannot meet?”
If citizens participate and nothing happens, trust may decrease. The process must set expectations clearly: some conversations inform policy, some track accountability, some support self-organisation, and some generate future priorities.

9. Best-Fit Customer Profile
The ideal first customer is:
A forward-looking municipality, city, or local civic coalition led by people who genuinely want better governance, are willing to listen publicly, and can use structured citizen insight to improve accountability, planning, collaboration, or community action.
Best-fit customers are likely to have several of these characteristics:
   * A mayor, councillor, civic innovation lead, or local organiser with credibility and courage.
   * A real local issue or set of issues around which people can gather.
   * Enough trust for participation to begin, but enough frustration for the need to be visible.
   * A desire to improve citizen engagement beyond surveys and town halls.
   * Some budget, sponsorship route, or partnership model.
   * A territory where local identity still matters.
   * Civil society actors who can help activate participation.
   * A willingness to pilot at manageable scale before expanding.
The strongest early use cases may include:
   * A municipality wanting to track progress against its electoral programme.
   * A city wanting to identify citizen priorities for the next mandate.
   * A local government wanting to coordinate businesses, NGOs, and citizens around a shared challenge.
   * A civic organisation wanting to build a citizen agenda independently from the elected power.
   * A sponsor-backed thematic conversation around sport, youth, loneliness, food, environment, animals, culture.
   * A local community recovering from an environmental or social disruption.
The first buyer is not necessarily the largest city. A smaller or mid-sized municipality with courageous leadership may be a much better early fit than a large bureaucracy with low appetite for experimentation.

10. Poor-Fit Customers
Poor-fit customers include:
   * Municipalities that only want a symbolic participation exercise.
   * Political actors looking to manipulate citizen conversations for campaign advantage.
   * Governments unwilling to hear criticism or show any accountability.
   * Communities where there is no trusted local partner and no realistic participation pathway.
   * Organisations seeking only a survey tool or basic consultation platform.
   * Public bodies with no capacity to act on insights.
   * Sponsors looking only for brand visibility, not genuine civic contribution.
   * Highly polarised contexts where the process would immediately become a proxy battlefield unless strong facilitation and governance are in place.
   * Customers who expect AI to solve political legitimacy automatically.
   * Groups who want “the voice of the people” as a simplistic mandate, without acknowledging participation bias and complexity.
Also poor fit: any customer who wants to use the platform to create the appearance of listening while decisions have already been made. That would damage trust and weaken The Coherence Company’s positioning.

12. Segment Summary
This segment is attractive because local democracy has a clear gap: people vote, but they are not continuously heard; politicians promise, but progress is not systematically understood; communities care, but often lack the infrastructure to find one another and act together.
The opportunity is not to replace municipal government. It is to make local governance more intelligent, more accountable, more participatory, and more capable of mobilising civil society.
Now is the right time because AI can help process many voices without reducing them to crude polling. It can support structured listening, thematic synthesis, matchmaking between people and organisations, progress tracking, and community self-organisation. At the same time, trust in institutions is fragile, local problems are complex, and many governments are reaching the limits of what they can do through spending and regulation alone.
The Coherence Company must prove several things to win this market:
   1. Citizens will participate when the process feels meaningful, safe, and useful.
   2. Municipalities or sponsors will pay for civic sensemaking when it produces actionable insight.
   3. The process can avoid political capture and maintain trust across different groups.
   4. AI synthesis can handle nuance without pretending to be neutral, omniscient, or democratically representative by itself.
   5. The conversations can lead not only to better understanding, but to better coordination and action.
The strategic wedge should be modest but meaningful: start with a pilot municipality or a well-defined civic theme, create a structured Coherence Conversation process, generate clear outputs for both leaders and citizens, and show that the community becomes more capable as a result.
The deeper potential is much larger: local government evolving from a distant provider of rules and services into a facilitator of civic intelligence, trust, and collaboration. In that future, civil society is not merely consulted. It becomes more able to see itself, organise itself, and act for the common good.
